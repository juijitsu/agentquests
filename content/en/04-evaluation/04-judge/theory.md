# Theory · The author as judge

For three levels running we built the measurement and fixed everything in it that
code could fix: a set appeared, semantic checking appeared, a breakdown by kind
appeared.

Now the measurement runs into whoever is judging.

## Six of six

The agent runs the set and grades its own answers. The result: six of six.

Two of those six answers:

```
expected: 18:00   answer: sometime in the evening
expected: 2.90    answer: around three dollars
```

"Sometime in the evening" is not 18:00. "Around three dollars" is not 2.90, and a
ten-cent difference per mile on a three thousand mile run comes to three hundred
dollars.

The author scored both as correct.

## Why an author is always softer

Not because they cheat. Because they **know what they meant to say.**

Reading "sometime in the evening", the author sees behind that phrase the very
eighteen hundred hours they had in mind. For them there is no distance between
intent and wording: they look at their answer and see their thought.

A judge who did not write the answer sees only the text. And that text does not
answer the question "at what time".

This is a property of the role, not of the model. A person reviewing their own
code finds fewer bugs for exactly the same reason: they read not what they wrote
but what they meant to write.

## What "an independent judge" means

Not "a different model" — though that helps too. What matters is something else:
**the judge must not know whose answer it is grading.**

The moment the grader knows it is looking at the new version everyone is waiting
for — or at an answer it wrote itself half an hour ago — the grade stops being a
measurement and becomes a confirmation.

Technically this is arranged simply: the judge is handed a rule and two texts, the
expected answer and the received one. No version, no author, no history of the
question.

## Why the judge needs a rule

The second thing without which a judge is useless: **a rubric**.

```
An answer counts if it names a specific value.
A range, an approximation and an evasive wording do not count.
```

Without one, every judge judges its own way, and yesterday's seventy percent is
incomparable with today's. The rubric is what makes two grades comparable: it
fixes what counts as correct before anyone looked at the answers.

It also protects you from argument. "But 'around three dollars' is almost right"
is a conversation that ends the moment the rule was written down in advance.

## The judge is wrong too

Do not imagine an independent judge is infallible. It is also a model, it is also
wrong, and its mistakes also have to be measured — usually by comparing its
verdicts with human ones on a small sample.

But its mistakes at least are **not biased in one direction**. An author is
always wrong in their own favour, and no number of runs fixes that: a biased
measurement does not become more accurate through repetition.
